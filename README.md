## Stretch Feature (declared before building)

`GET /content/<content_id>` returns one item's current state: its original
decision plus every appeal against it, and the status the latest line leaves
it in. A writer who appealed can check where their appeal stands without
reading the whole log.

Built as declared, with one change: the response gives the number of appeals
and the time of the latest one, not the appeals themselves. The appeal
reasoning is the writer's message to a reviewer, not something every reader of
the post should see.

The plan written before any code (the path, both signals and their blind
spots, the false-positive trace and the endpoint contract) is in
[docs/trace.md](docs/trace.md).

---

## What This Does

Provenance Guard takes a piece of writing and a creator id, decides whether it
reads as written by a person, generated by AI, or too close to call, and says
so in a full sentence a reader can understand. Every decision, every appeal
and every refused request is written to an append-only log.

1. **The route checks the body** (`app.py::submit`). It must be a JSON object
   with a non-empty `text` string and a `creator_id` string. The text is
   trimmed and must then be at most 20,000 characters and at least 30 words.
   A bad body gets a 4xx with a JSON `error` and a `rejected` log line. It
   never reaches a model.
2. **Signal one** (`detector.py::model_signal`). GPT-2 measures how
   predictable the first 400 tokens are.
3. **Signal two** (`stylometry.py::style_signal`). Three counts of the text's
   shape: how much sentence length varies, how varied the vocabulary is, and
   how much uncommon punctuation it uses.
4. **The combining rule** (`scoring.py::combine_signals`). The two scores
   become one number from 0 (confidently human) to 1 (confidently AI).
5. **The label** (`scoring.py::score_to_label`). That number maps to `human`,
   `unsure` or `ai` and to the sentence a reader sees.
6. **The log and the answer.** The route mints a `content_id` (a UUID) and
   writes one decision line to `logs/audit.jsonl` with both signal scores,
   the combined score, the guess, the label and `status: decided`. The same
   fields go back to the caller as JSON.
7. **The appeal.** A writer who disagrees sends `POST /appeal` with the
   `content_id` and their reasoning. The service finds the decision in the
   log, appends an appeal line with `status: under_review` and confirms. The
   decision line is never edited. `GET /content/<content_id>` shows where the
   item stands now.

## Signals and Scoring

Every number below was measured on an 18-text calibration set: 10 human texts
published before ChatGPT existed (r/rutgers posts, Simple Wikipedia,
Wikipedia, an arXiv abstract), 6 unedited AI texts and 2 lightly edited ones.
The texts, sources and dates are in `samples/calibration.json`, and the full
tables are in [docs/calibration.md](docs/calibration.md).

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** perplexity under GPT-2 on the first 400 tokens, which is
how surprised the model is, on average, by each next token. `model_signal`
squashes it onto 0 to 1 with a logistic on log-perplexity centred at
perplexity 25 with steepness 1.6. Lower perplexity gives a higher score, and
higher means more AI-like.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** a language model
writes by picking likely next words, so its output tends to be easy for
another model to predict. People take odd turns and use specific names and
phrasings a model would not pick.

**What it can't see:** who wrote the text. It scores how plain and
conventional the wording is, and on real data that is most of what it sees.
At the starter's midpoint (45) it did not separate the calibration groups at
all: mean 0.713 for human text and 0.719 for AI text. The human Wikipedia
paragraph on the Erie Canal was more predictable to GPT-2 (perplexity 14.5)
than an AI paragraph on the same facts (19.2). Simple, encyclopedic or
second-language writing looks like a model to it. AI text full of jargon
looks human: the AI paper abstract scored 0.28. GPT-2 is a 2019 model that
never saw the chat models it is judging, and it reads only the first 400
tokens, so anything after roughly 300 words is invisible to it.

The midpoint moved from 45 to 25, the median perplexity of the ten human
calibration texts. At 45 every human text scored above 0.5, so ordinary human
writing started out leaning AI. At 25 a typical human text is a coin flip on
this signal.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** three counts of the text's shape. Each one becomes a
0 to 1 AI-likeness by a straight-line ramp between two anchors in
`config.py`, and the three are averaged with equal weight.

| Measure | Reads human at | Reads AI at |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence-length spread (coefficient of variation of words per sentence) | 0.60 or more | 0.25 or less |
| Vocabulary variety (moving-average type-token ratio, 50-word window) | 0.75 or less | 0.90 or more |
| Uncommon punctuation per word: `; : ! ? ( ) " … / & *` and curly quotes | 0.045 or more | 0.005 or less |

A text with fewer than three sentences has no spread to measure, so that
measure counts as 0.5 (no evidence either way).

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** generated prose tends
to come out in even, mid-length sentences with tidy punctuation and little
repetition. People put a three-word sentence beside a forty-word one, repeat
themselves, and reach for parentheses and question marks unevenly. The
vocabulary measure runs the opposite way from the starter's hint. On the
calibration set the AI texts repeated fewer words (mean MATTR 0.86) than the
human ones (0.72 to 0.80), because current chat models avoid repeating
themselves and people writing a forum post do not. Em dashes are left out of
the punctuation count on purpose: current chat models overuse them, so
counting them as human would reward AI text.

**What it can't see:** meaning. It never reads the words, it only counts
them. Anything that changes the shape without changing who wrote it moves the
score: splitting a sentence in two, adding a typo, adding a parenthesis or a
question mark, pasting in a list. Plain human writing has the same even
sentences and sparse punctuation as AI text, so both Simple Wikipedia texts
land near 0.5. Short texts give it very little to count.

### The combining rule

**The rule:** a weighted average in which the style signal counts twice as
much as the model signal, followed by an agreement gate on the AI side. If
the average reaches the AI threshold while either signal on its own is at or
below 0.5, the score is capped at 0.69, just under the AI line, and the text
is labelled unsure. One signal alone never produces the AI label. The gate
only guards the AI side. A low average gets the human label without both
signals agreeing, because a wrong "human" costs the site a little and a wrong
"AI" costs a person a lot. The bands are lopsided for the same reason.

**The numbers:**

- `combined = 1/3 × model_score + 2/3 × style_score`. Style carries more
  because it separated the calibration groups and the model signal did not.
  The model signal keeps a third because it reads word choice in context,
  which style cannot see.
- Agreement floor 0.5. The AI label needs `min(model_score, style_score) > 0.5`.
  Otherwise an average of 0.70 or more is capped at 0.69.
- Bands: human below 0.45, unsure from 0.45 up to 0.70, AI at 0.70 and above.
  Reaching AI takes 0.20 above a coin flip. Reaching human takes 0.05 below it.
- Every number lives in `config.py` with its reason. The rule was written down
  in docs/calibration.md before it was coded.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals` (the rule) and
`scoring.py::score_to_label` (the bands).

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:** an
AI-written paper abstract from the calibration set. GPT-2 finds its jargon
surprising (perplexity 45.4, `model_score` 0.28). Its shape is the most
machine-like in the set (`style_score` 0.96: even sentences, MATTR 0.94, no
uncommon punctuation at all). The weighted average is 0.73, which would be the
AI label. The gate sees the model signal at 0.28, caps the score at 0.69, and
the abstract is labelled unsure. That is a miss on a real AI text, and the
rule makes it on purpose. Style is also the signal that flags plain human
writing, so it does not get to accuse anyone by itself.

The opposite worry is a real 2019 r/rutgers post ("got a harsh reality
check"). It is human, and both signals lean AI (model 0.63, style 0.64), so the
gate cannot help. Its average is 0.636, under 0.70, so it is labelled unsure.
That 0.064 margin is the thinnest on the human side of the calibration set
and the number to watch in unit 8.

**Where this leaves the criteria before unit 8 runs:** on the calibration set
the final rule gives the AI label to 0 of 10 human texts (7 human, 3 unsure)
and to 2 of 6 unedited AI texts. Three of the four AI texts it missed sit
within 0.01 of the line (0.690, 0.693, 0.697), and the mean gap between the
groups is 0.27. If unit 8's Set A behaves the same way, criterion 2 (3 of 5
flagged and a 0.30 gap) misses. The 0.70 line was not lowered to chase those
three. The rule was written before these numbers existed, and lowering the
line would cut the human margin from 0.064 to about 0.04 on the strength of
18 texts.

## Label Variants

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 up to 0.45 | Every post here gets an automated check for AI-generated writing. This one found nothing that points to AI, so we think a person wrote it. |
| unsure | 0.45 up to 0.70 | Our automated check couldn't tell whether a person or an AI wrote this. That happens with a lot of real writing, especially plain or very polished writing. It is not a finding against the writer. Nothing needs to change, and the post stays up as normal. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.70 to 1.00 | Our automated check flagged this text as likely AI-generated. Checks like this can be wrong, including about writing people did themselves. If you wrote this, you can ask for a person to review it. |

Each boundary belongs to the band above it: 0.45 is unsure and 0.70 is AI.
Every score from 0 to 1 gets exactly one label (`tests/test_labels.py` walks
all 1,001 steps of 0.001). All three are reached by real texts in the Sample
Run below.

Once a writer appeals, `GET /content/<content_id>` shows this in place of the
original label:

> The writer has asked for a person to review the automated flag on this
> post. Until that review is finished, the flag is unconfirmed and is not a
> finding against the writer.

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:** the person reading it may well
have written every word. On the calibration set the human texts that land
here are all plain writing: both Simple Wikipedia texts and the "harsh reality
check" post. So it says what happened in plain words
("couldn't tell"), names the kinds of writing this happens to so the writer
knows it is common, and says outright that it is not a finding against them
and that the post stays up. The "Nothing needs to change" sentence came from
a read-back (How I Used AI, moment 1). Without it, the draft left a careful
writer thinking they should make their writing messier next time, and that is
the wrong lesson to teach. It never shows a number, because a score beside
"couldn't tell" reads like a near miss.

The reader never sees the words "high-confidence". Those are band names for
this README. The AI label says "likely", because a reader told "high
confidence" has been told not to doubt it.

## How I Used AI

Claude Code (Anthropic's coding agent) built this unit at my request. That
covers the code, the tests, the calibration set, docs/trace.md, criteria.md
and this README, including the five criteria. The 87 pytest tests and the live
run in Sample Run were run by Claude Code. The two moments below are the
points where a second AI agent, given none of the build's context, read the
work as an outsider, and its answer changed what shipped.

**Moment 1**

- *What I asked for:* Claude Code gave a fresh agent the three draft labels
  and asked how each would read to the writer whose work got it: what they
  would feel, what they would conclude about themselves, and what they would
  do next. The AI draft said the check "found strong signs that this text may
  have been generated by AI". The unsure draft did not have the "Nothing needs
  to change" sentence.
- *What came back:* the AI label read as an accusation even with the hedge
  after it ("'Strong signs' is the phrase that stings, and I'd read it as
  'they think I cheated'"). The writer would appeal at once, but worried that
  a "review pending" label would look like an admission to other readers. The
  unsure label read as "unresolved, not accused", but "a careful writer might
  wonder if they should roughen their style next time, which would be a bad
  lesson to take away". The human label raised the question of why their post
  was being checked at all.
- *What I changed:* the AI label now says the check "flagged this text as
  likely AI-generated" and offers a person's review in the same breath. The
  unsure label gained "Nothing needs to change". The human label opens by
  saying every post gets the check, so no one reads it as being singled out.
  The review-pending text that `GET /content` shows after an appeal says the
  flag is "unconfirmed" and "not a finding against the writer", so a pending
  review does not read as guilt.

**Moment 2**

- *What I asked for:* Claude Code gave a second fresh agent criterion 1 (0 of
  10 pre-ChatGPT human pieces get the AI label, in each of three trials) and
  asked for the message a writer would send after being wrongly flagged
  anyway.
- *What came back:* a letter from a writer with a plain, formal style who had
  their drafts and timestamps. It pointed out that "high-confidence" tells
  readers not to bother doubting the flag. It named second-language writers as
  the people this will keep happening to. It asked for four things: a human
  reviewer, the reasons in plain terms, a note on their record if the flag is
  withdrawn, and a statement of what changes for people in their position.
- *What I changed:* two things, and two gaps named instead of hidden.
  1. The reader-facing AI label never says "high-confidence". It says
     "likely".
  2. This README now says what 0 of 10 does and does not mean. GPT-2 and the
     style counts are deterministic, so the three trials run the same ten texts
     to the same scores. They are one observation repeated, not thirty. With 0
     failures in 10, the true rate of wrongly flagging human writing could
     still be as high as about 26% at 95% confidence (the exact binomial bound
     is 1 − 0.05^(1/10) = 0.259, and the rule of three gives 30%). Reading
     "0 of 10" as "never" asks more of ten texts than they can carry.

  Not built in unit 7: plain-language reasons for a writer, and a recorded
  outcome when a review withdraws a flag. `GET /content` shows both signal
  scores, which a reviewer can use, but a writer still gets no sentence about
  why. The calibration set also has no second-language writing, so the case
  the letter names most directly is untested here. Both go to unit 8's "What's
  Still Broken" if the attack set confirms them.
