## Stretch Feature (declared before building)

`GET /content/<content_id>` returns one item's current state: its original
decision plus every appeal against it, and the status the latest line leaves
it in. A writer who appealed can check where their appeal stands without
reading the whole log.

Built as declared, with one change: the response gives the number of appeals
and the time of the latest one, not the appeals themselves. The appeal
reasoning is the writer's message to a reviewer, not something every reader of
the post should see.

Two limits of that. The starter's `GET /log` is an operator's view and still
returns every line, reasoning included. And there are no accounts in unit 7,
so anyone holding a `content_id` can appeal it. The appeal line records the
creator from the original decision, not who sent the appeal.

The plan written before any code (the path, both signals and their blind
spots, the false-positive trace and the endpoint contract) is in
[docs/trace.md](docs/trace.md).

## What This Does

Provenance Guard takes a piece of writing and a creator id, decides whether it
reads as written by a person, generated by AI, or too close to call, and says
so in a full sentence a reader can understand. Every decision, every appeal
and every refused request is written to an append-only log.

1. **The route checks the body** (`app.py::submit`). It must be a JSON object
   with a non-empty `text` string and a `creator_id` string. The text is
   trimmed and must then be at most 20,000 characters and at least 30 words.
   A bad body gets a 4xx with a JSON `error` and a `rejected` log line. It
   never reaches a model.
2. **Signal one** (`detector.py::model_signal`). GPT-2 measures how
   predictable the first 400 tokens are.
3. **Signal two** (`stylometry.py::style_signal`). Three counts of the text's
   shape: how much sentence length varies, how varied the vocabulary is, and
   how much uncommon punctuation it uses.
4. **The combining rule** (`scoring.py::combine_signals`). The two scores
   become one number from 0 (confidently human) to 1 (confidently AI).
5. **The label** (`scoring.py::score_to_label`). That number maps to `human`,
   `unsure` or `ai` and to the sentence a reader sees.
6. **The log and the answer.** The route mints a `content_id` (a UUID) and
   writes one decision line to `logs/audit.jsonl` with both signal scores,
   the combined score, the guess, the label and `status: decided`. The same
   fields go back to the caller as JSON.
7. **The appeal.** A writer who disagrees sends `POST /appeal` with the
   `content_id` and their reasoning. The service finds the decision in the
   log, appends an appeal line with `status: under_review` and confirms. The
   decision line is never edited. `GET /content/<content_id>` shows where the
   item stands now.

## Signals and Scoring

Every number below was measured on an 18-text calibration set: 10 human texts
published before ChatGPT existed (r/rutgers posts, Simple Wikipedia,
Wikipedia, an arXiv abstract), 6 unedited AI texts and 2 lightly edited ones.
The texts, sources and dates are in `samples/calibration.json`, and the full
tables are in [docs/calibration.md](docs/calibration.md).

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** perplexity under GPT-2 on the first 400 tokens, which is
how surprised the model is, on average, by each next token. `model_signal`
squashes it onto 0 to 1 with a logistic on log-perplexity centred at
perplexity 25 with steepness 1.6. Lower perplexity gives a higher score, and
higher means more AI-like.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** a language model
writes by picking likely next words, so its output tends to be easy for
another model to predict. People take odd turns and use specific names and
phrasings a model would not pick.

**What it can't see:** who wrote the text. It scores how plain and
conventional the wording is, and on real data that is most of what it sees.
At the starter's midpoint (45) it did not separate the calibration groups at
all: mean 0.713 for human text and 0.719 for AI text. The human Wikipedia
paragraph on the Erie Canal was more predictable to GPT-2 (perplexity 14.5)
than an AI paragraph on the same facts (19.2). Simple, encyclopedic or
second-language writing looks like a model to it. AI text full of jargon
looks human: the AI paper abstract scored 0.28. GPT-2 is a 2019 model that
never saw the chat models it is judging, and it reads only the first 400
tokens, so anything after roughly 300 words is invisible to it.

The midpoint moved from 45 to 25, the median perplexity of the ten human
calibration texts. At 45 every human text scored above 0.5, so ordinary human
writing started out leaning AI. At 25 a typical human text is a coin flip on
this signal.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** three counts of the text's shape. Each one becomes a
0 to 1 AI-likeness by a straight-line ramp between two anchors in
`config.py`, and the three are averaged with equal weight.

| Measure | Reads human at | Reads AI at |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence-length spread (coefficient of variation of words per sentence) | 0.60 or more | 0.25 or less |
| Vocabulary variety (moving-average type-token ratio, 50-word window) | 0.75 or less | 0.90 or more |
| Uncommon punctuation per word: `; : ! ? ( ) " … / & *` and curly quotes | 0.045 or more | 0.005 or less |

A text with fewer than three sentences has no spread to measure, so that
measure counts as 0.5 (no evidence either way).

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** generated prose tends
to come out in even, mid-length sentences with tidy punctuation and little
repetition. People put a three-word sentence beside a forty-word one, repeat
themselves, and reach for parentheses and question marks unevenly. The
vocabulary measure runs the opposite way from the starter's hint. On the
calibration set the AI texts repeated fewer words (mean MATTR 0.86) than the
human ones (0.72 to 0.80), because current chat models avoid repeating
themselves and people writing a forum post do not. Em dashes are left out of
the punctuation count on purpose: current chat models overuse them, so
counting them as human would reward AI text.

**What it can't see:** meaning. It never reads the words, it only counts
them. Anything that changes the shape without changing who wrote it moves the
score: splitting a sentence in two, adding a typo, adding a parenthesis or a
question mark, pasting in a list. Plain human writing has the same even
sentences and sparse punctuation as AI text, so both Simple Wikipedia texts
land near 0.5. Short texts give it very little to count.

### The combining rule

**The rule:** a weighted average in which the style signal counts twice as
much as the model signal, followed by an agreement gate on the AI side. If
the average reaches the AI threshold while either signal on its own is at or
below 0.5, the score is capped 0.01 under the AI line (at 0.74 now, 0.69 in
unit 7), and the text is labelled unsure. One signal alone never produces the AI label. The gate
only guards the AI side. A low average gets the human label without both
signals agreeing, because a wrong "human" costs the site a little and a wrong
"AI" costs a person a lot. The bands are lopsided for the same reason.

**The numbers:**

- `combined = 1/3 × model_score + 2/3 × style_score`. Style carries more
  because it separated the calibration groups and the model signal did not.
  The model signal keeps a third because it reads word choice in context,
  which style cannot see.
- Agreement floor 0.5. The AI label needs `min(model_score, style_score) > 0.5`.
  Otherwise an average at or over the AI line is capped 0.01 under it.
- Bands: human below 0.45, unsure from 0.45 up to 0.75, AI at 0.75 and above.
  Reaching AI takes 0.25 above a coin flip. Reaching human takes 0.05 below it.
  The AI line was 0.70 in unit 7. Unit 8's one improvement raised it, for the
  reason in The Improvement.
- Every number lives in `config.py` with its reason. The rule was written down
  in docs/calibration.md before it was coded.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals` (the rule) and
`scoring.py::score_to_label` (the bands).

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:** an
AI-written paper abstract from the calibration set. GPT-2 finds its jargon
surprising (perplexity 45.4, `model_score` 0.28). Its shape is the most
machine-like in the set (`style_score` 0.96: even sentences, MATTR 0.94, no
uncommon punctuation at all). The weighted average is 0.73, which would be the
AI label. The gate sees the model signal at 0.28, caps the score at 0.69, and
the abstract is labelled unsure. That is a miss on a real AI text, and the
rule makes it on purpose. Style is also the signal that flags plain human
writing, so it does not get to accuse anyone by itself. That was the rule with
the line at 0.70. At 0.75 the abstract's 0.73 is unsure before the gate comes
into it, and the case the gate now holds is the staff attack set's AI paragraph
(model 0.44, style 1.0, average 0.81, capped at 0.74).

The opposite worry is a real 2019 r/rutgers post ("got a harsh reality
check"). It is human, and both signals lean AI (model 0.63, style 0.64), so the
gate cannot help. Its average is 0.636, so it is labelled unsure. Under unit
7's 0.70 line that was a 0.064 margin, the thinnest on the human side of the
calibration set and the number unit 8 had to watch (at 0.75 it is 0.114).
Unit 8 found a thinner one: FP03 in the
staff attack set, careful second-language writing, at 0.7034, over the 0.70
line (Attack Run — Before).

**Where this leaves the criteria before unit 8 runs:** on the calibration set
the final rule gives the AI label to 0 of 10 human texts (7 human, 3 unsure)
and to 2 of 6 unedited AI texts. The four it missed score 0.693, 0.690, 0.648
and 0.454. The 0.690 is the abstract held at the gate's cap. The mean gap
between the groups is 0.27. If unit 8's Set A behaves the same way, criterion
2 (3 of 5 flagged and a 0.30 gap) misses. The 0.70 line was not lowered to
chase the 0.693 text. The rule was written before these numbers existed, and
catching that one text would take a line at 0.693, which cuts the human
margin from 0.064 to 0.057. The abstract stays unsure under any line, because
the cap moves with it.

## Label Variants

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 up to 0.45 | Every post here gets an automated check for AI-generated writing. This one found nothing that points to AI, so we think a person wrote it. |
| unsure | 0.45 up to 0.75 | Our automated check couldn't tell whether a person or an AI wrote this. That happens with a lot of real writing, especially plain or very polished writing. It is not a finding against the writer. Nothing needs to change, and the post stays up as normal. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.75 to 1.00 | Our automated check flagged this text as likely AI-generated. Checks like this can be wrong, including about writing people did themselves. If you wrote this, you can ask for a person to review it. |

Each boundary belongs to the band above it: 0.45 is unsure and 0.75 is AI.
The AI band started at 0.70 until unit 8's improvement.
Every score from 0 to 1 gets exactly one label (`tests/test_labels.py` walks
all 1,001 steps of 0.001). All three are reached by real texts in the Sample
Run below.

Once a writer appeals an AI label, `GET /content/<content_id>` shows this in
its place. A human or unsure label already says it is not a finding against
the writer, so it stays up while a review is pending:

> The writer has asked for a person to review the automated flag on this
> post. Until that review is finished, the flag is unconfirmed and is not a
> finding against the writer.

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:** the person reading it may well
have written every word. On the calibration set the human texts that land
here are all plain writing: both Simple Wikipedia texts and the "harsh reality
check" post. So it says what happened in plain words
("couldn't tell"), names the kinds of writing this happens to so the writer
knows it is common, and says outright that it is not a finding against them
and that the post stays up. The "Nothing needs to change" sentence came from
a read-back (How I Used AI, moment 1). Without it, the draft left a careful
writer thinking they should make their writing messier next time, and that is
the wrong lesson to teach. It never shows a number, because a score beside
"couldn't tell" reads like a near miss.

The reader never sees the words "high-confidence". Those are band names for
this README. The AI label says "likely", because a reader told "high
confidence" has been told not to doubt it.

## How I Used AI

Claude Code (Anthropic's coding agent) built this unit at my request. That
covers the code, the tests, the calibration set, docs/trace.md, criteria.md
and this README, including the five criteria. The 95 pytest tests and the live
run in Sample Run were run by Claude Code. The two moments below are the
points where a second AI agent, given none of the build's context, read the
work as an outsider, and its answer changed what shipped. A separate review
agent read the finished code before it was pushed and found a rounding hole:
an average of 0.69997 slipped past the agreement gate and then rounded up to
0.7000, so one signal alone could reach the AI label. It was fixed test-first
(commit `cc155be`).

**Moment 1**

- *What I asked for:* Claude Code gave a fresh agent the three draft labels
  and asked how each would read to the writer whose work got it: what they
  would feel, what they would conclude about themselves, and what they would
  do next. The AI draft said the check "found strong signs that this text may
  have been generated by AI". The unsure draft did not have the "Nothing needs
  to change" sentence.
- *What came back:* the AI label read as an accusation even with the hedge
  after it ("'Strong signs' is the phrase that stings, and I'd read it as
  'they think I cheated'"). The writer would appeal at once, but worried that
  a "review pending" label would look like an admission to other readers. The
  unsure label read as "unresolved, not accused", but "a careful writer might
  wonder if they should roughen their style next time, which would be a bad
  lesson to take away". The human label raised the question of why their post
  was being checked at all.
- *What I changed:* the AI label now says the check "flagged this text as
  likely AI-generated" and offers a person's review in the same breath. The
  unsure label gained "Nothing needs to change". The human label opens by
  saying every post gets the check, so no one reads it as being singled out.
  The review-pending text that `GET /content` shows after an appeal says the
  flag is "unconfirmed" and "not a finding against the writer", so a pending
  review does not read as guilt.

**Moment 2**

- *What I asked for:* Claude Code gave a second fresh agent criterion 1 (0 of
  10 pre-ChatGPT human pieces get the AI label, in each of three trials) and
  asked for the message a writer would send after being wrongly flagged
  anyway.
- *What came back:* a letter from a writer with a plain, formal style who had
  their drafts and timestamps. It pointed out that "high-confidence" tells
  readers not to bother doubting the flag. It named second-language writers as
  the people this will keep happening to. It asked for four things: a human
  reviewer, the reasons in plain terms, a note on their record if the flag is
  withdrawn, and a statement of what changes for people in their position.
- *What I changed:* two things, and two gaps named instead of hidden.
  1. The reader-facing AI label never says "high-confidence". It says
     "likely".
  2. This README now says what 0 of 10 does and does not mean. GPT-2 and the
     style counts are deterministic, so the three trials run the same ten texts
     to the same scores. They are one observation repeated, not thirty. With 0
     failures in 10, the true rate of wrongly flagging human writing could
     still be as high as about 26% at 95% confidence (the exact binomial bound
     is 1 − 0.05^(1/10) = 0.259, and the rule of three gives 30%). Reading
     "0 of 10" as "never" asks more of ten texts than they can carry.

  Not built in unit 7: plain-language reasons for a writer, and a recorded
  outcome when a review withdraws a flag. `GET /content` shows both signal
  scores, which a reviewer can use, but a writer still gets no sentence about
  why. The calibration set also has no second-language writing, so the case
  the letter names most directly is untested here. Both go to unit 8's "What's
  Still Broken" if the attack set confirms them.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 5 per minute, 50 per day

**Why those numbers and not others:**

A writer posting their own work submits a piece, maybe spots a typo, and
submits it again. Five in one minute leaves room for that plus a double-click.
Fifty in a day is more than anyone posts by hand.

A script mapping my bands is the opposite case. Every response hands back the
combined score and both signal scores, so each probe tells the script a lot.
It still needs hundreds of them to find where 0.45 and 0.75 sit for the kind
of text it wants to pass. At fifty a day, one id gets fifty probes. The
starter's 200 a day would have given it four times that.

The two limits do different jobs. The minute limit stops a burst. The day
limit stops a patient script: five a minute alone still allows 7,200 a day to
anything that sends one every twelve seconds.

The window is a moving one (`RATE_LIMIT_STRATEGY = "moving-window"` in
`config.py`). A fixed window resets on the minute, which lets a script send
five at 0:59 and five more at 1:00.

**What a caller counts as:** the `creator_id` in the request body, cleaned the
same way the log cleans it, so `" bob"` and `"bob "` share bob's budget. When a
body has no usable `creator_id` (the field is missing, blank, not a string or
over 200 characters, or the body isn't a JSON object), it counts against the
caller's address instead.

The two choices fail in different ways. `creator_id` is whatever the caller
types, so a script that changes it on every request gets a fresh budget each
time. Against that script, this limit does nothing. Counting per address would
stop it, but would put a whole dorm network behind one budget. On this laptop
it would also put every caller behind `127.0.0.1`, so `run_eval.py` would
throttle itself. There are no accounts in this service, so per-creator is the
only identity I have that belongs to a writer. That means id rotation walks
straight past the limit, which is listed under What's Still Broken.

Refused requests count against the budget too. Flask-Limiter checks the limit
before `/submit` runs, so a request that would have got a `422` still uses up
one of the five. A body over 256 KB can't be read for its `creator_id`, so it
counts against the address, and the sixth one in a minute gets a `429` instead
of a `413`.

A code review of this section found three holes in my first version, all now
fixed and covered by tests in `tests/test_rate_limit.py`:

1. The limiter used the raw id while the log stripped it, so `"bob"` with
   different padding got a fresh budget each time and the log showed them all
   as `bob`.
2. An id of any length became a limiter key, kept for a day.
3. Reading an oversized body raised before the limiter counted it, so 413s
   never turned into 429s.

The `429` line also used to log the raw id. It now logs the cleaned one, with
the route and status like every other refusal.

  that only signal two reads as AI, and it let FP03 through because signal one
  voted 0.6173, over the 0.5 floor. The same vote also raised the average.
  With a model score at or under 0.5, FP03's average would be at most 0.664,
  unsure before the gate had anything to do.

So it was cause 1, signal one: GPT-2 reads predictable wording as AI, and
careful second-language English is very predictable to it (perplexity 18.5
against 29.0 for the AI paragraph). That wrong vote opened the gate. Signal two
adds to it through the punctuation measure, which gives 1.000 to FP01 through
FP07 and to the control alike. On plain text that measure carries no
information and adds a flat third to the style score. Without it, FP03's style
score would be 0.620 and its combined score 0.619. Signal two still ranks this
writer below AI text, though, and signal one ranks them above it.

**Diagnoses**

The two misses are one problem seen twice. Nothing out of the 20 texts reached
the AI band: the highest score was 0.6544, and the band started at 0.70 for
this run.

Criterion 2, missed. The stage is signal one, and the combining rule turns its
failure into a veto.

- GPT-2 rates a text as AI when the text is predictable to GPT-2.
  `claude-sonnet-5-5` does not write like a 2019 model. Its casual comments
  were less predictable to GPT-2 than the real 2019 Hacker News comments:
  mean model score 0.274 for the three casual Set A texts against 0.360 for
  the five casual Set H texts. In the formal register the two means were the
  same to four places (0.5163 and 0.5163). Across all of Set A the model
  signal pointed the wrong way, at 0.371 against 0.438 for Set H.
- The combining rule gives that signal a third of the weight, so a low model
  score caps what style can do. A1 had the strongest style score in Set A at
  0.776, but with a model score of 0.189 it needed 0.956 to reach 0.70. The
  agreement gate also needs the model score above 0.5, which no casual Set A
  text had. A5 (the abstract) was the only one past 0.5 on the model, and its
  style score was 0.461. The `Background:` / `Methods:` labels and `(FDA)` count
  as uncommon punctuation, which my style signal reads as human.
- So 0 of 5 could reach `ai`. The +0.051 gap splits into +0.073 from style
  (Set A did lean AI on style, 0.597 against 0.488) and −0.022 from the model
  signal pulling the other way.
- The gate does the opposite of its job on this data. Two human texts had both
  signals above 0.5 (H7, an encyclopedia preamble at 0.694 and 0.583, and H10,
  a PubMed abstract at 0.510 and 0.587). No Set A text did. If the AI band
  started at 0.61, the only one of the 20 texts to get the AI label would be
  H7, a human encyclopedia entry. Lowering the threshold makes this worse.

Unit 7's calibration warned about this. GPT-2 did not separate the groups there
either (0.713 against 0.719), and I said criterion 2 would likely miss. This
run shows the same thing on texts and a model the calibration never saw.

Criterion 3, missed on both halves.

- No `ai` label at all, for the reason above.
- 2 of 5 Set B landed `unsure`, against a target of 3. The other three went to
  `human`. The stage is signal two, and the measure is sentence-length
  spread. Over a text of 6 to 9 sentences, the coefficient of variation is set
  by its shortest and longest sentences, so one split or merge swings it.
  Splitting "We identified all new oncology drugs ... and obtained their launch
  prices" in B5 created a five-word sentence. The spread rose from 0.394 to
  0.559, the style score fell from 0.461 to 0.297, and the label went from

- The typos barely touched vocabulary variety (MATTR moved 0.003 at most). They
  did move signal one: a misspelled word is a rare token to GPT-2, so the model
  score dropped in all five pairs (A5 0.693 to B5 0.650, for example). Every
  typo pushes toward `human`.
- The pattern: light editing does not move AI text into the middle band. It
  moves it at random along one fragile measure, and the typos add a steady
  push toward human. Set A, the unedited AI text, landed `unsure` 5 of 5,

## The Improvement

**What I changed:** `AI_THRESHOLD` in `config.py`, from 0.70 to 0.75. Nothing
else in the system moved. The gate's cap is `AI_THRESHOLD − 0.01`, so it went
from 0.69 to 0.74 with the line. The change was made test-first (commit
`3d57f16`). Two regression tests use logged numbers: FP03's (0.6173, 0.7464)
has to come out `unsure`, and the Sample Run essay's (0.6033, 0.837) has to
stay `ai`.

**Which diagnosis pointed at it:** the worst outcome, FP03. Real human writing
landed in the AI band at 0.7034. The diagnosis puts the fault in signal one,
and the fix it names is a reference model trained on text recent enough that
modern AI writing is predictable to it. That means replacing GPT-2, refitting the logistic's midpoint
and redoing the calibration, with new held-out test sets after it. That is
more than one change. Moving the floor of the AI band is the one change that
stops the accusation with the parts already here.

The rule for the number was to put the band above every human text this
service has scored. The highest are FP03 at 0.7034, the calibration set's
"harsh reality check" at 0.636, Set H's H5 at 0.634 and the substitute set's
FP01 at 0.567. 0.75 clears FP03 by 0.047. The line was picked after seeing
FP03, so the after run is in-sample for that row: it shows the change does
what the arithmetic says, and it can't show that the change generalizes.

Two other changes were considered and dropped.

- Equal weights. FP03 falls to 0.6819, and EV10 rises to 0.739 with both
  signals over 0.5, so it would be flagged. But equal weights move weight onto
  signal one, the signal the diagnoses found unreliable on both sides, and
  EV10 would be caught for its repetition, not for being AI text.
- Dropping the punctuation measure. FP03's combined score falls to 0.619 and
  the AI paragraph keeps its style score of 1.0. But punctuation is the one
  measure that pulls a writer who uses semicolons, parentheses and questions
  toward human, every text's style score would move, and it would need its own
  calibration pass.

## What's Still Broken

Two reasons for stopping come up again and again, so they are stated once
here. The unit allows one change to the system beyond the rate limiter and the
log, and that went to the AI line. And any change to a signal moves every
score, so it needs a new calibration pass and new held-out test sets after it.

**Attacks still getting through**

- **Evasion, EV01 to EV07 and EV09, and criterion 2 (signal one).** The
  detector can't flag the staff set's AI paragraph even unedited, and no Set A
  text got `ai`. What
  I'd do: replace GPT-2 as the reference model with one trained on recent
  text, refit `MODEL_PPL_MIDPOINT` on a fresh calibration set, and keep the
  gate. Why I stopped: it is a second change to scoring and needs the full
  calibration redone.
- **Dilution, PI01 to PI03 (signal one).** Unusual text in front of AI text
  pulls the model score down. What I'd do: score each paragraph on its own and
  take the most AI-like one that is long enough to score, so a preamble can't
  dilute the body. Why I stopped: on this set it reaches one row of three.
  PI03's AI paragraph is 33 words and could be scored alone, PI02's is 27,
  under the 30-word floor, and PI01 has no paragraph break at all. It also
  changes what both signals see, so it needs its own calibration pass.
- **Repetition, EV10 (signal two).** Repeated text drives vocabulary variety
  down, and my anchors read low variety as human. What I'd do: drop repeated
  sentences before measuring style. Why I stopped: the one-change rule.
- **The punctuation measure.** It gives 1.000 to every plain text, human or
  AI, so on plain text it adds a flat third to the style score. It is part of
  why FP03's style score was high. What I'd do: score zero uncommon
  punctuation as no evidence (0.5), then rerun calibration to see what that
  does to human texts that use punctuation. Why I stopped: the one-change
  rule, and it moves every style score.

**Criteria still missed**

- **Criterion 2:** the evasion item above. Raising the line moved it further
  away.
- **Criterion 3:** no `ai` label in the eval, for criterion 2's reason, and
  Set B moves at random along the spread measure, where one split or merged
  sentence swings the coefficient of variation. What I'd do: measure spread
  with something one sentence can't swing, such as the interquartile range of
  sentence lengths. Why I stopped: the one-change rule.
- **Criterion 1** is met, and still met by a detector that said `ai` to
  nothing in the eval. That stays true after the change.

**Holes the runs found outside the detector**

- **Two requests crash the route and leave no log line** (substitute MR16 and
  MR17). A lone surrogate makes the tokenizer raise `TypeError`, and deep
  nesting makes `json.loads` raise `RecursionError`. What I'd do: refuse text
  that can't be encoded as UTF-8 with a `422` before scoring, and parse the
  body in one shared helper that turns `RecursionError` into `400
  invalid_json`, both with a `rejected` line. Catching it in `_json_body`
  alone wouldn't work: Flask keeps nothing from a failed parse, so the route
  would parse the body again and crash there instead. Why I stopped: that is input validation, a second system
  change. It is the first thing I'd fix next, since these are the only
  requests in any run that leave no trace in the log.
- **Non-English text is refused as too short** (MF07, and the substitute's
  Japanese ED04, both 0 words). `stylometry.words` only matches the ASCII
  letters a to z, digits and apostrophes. What I'd do: refuse non-English text
  with a reason that says so, because GPT-2 and the style anchors were only
  calibrated on English and a label would mean nothing. Why I stopped: a route
  change, under the one-change rule.
- **Short real posts get no check** (FP08). Anything under 30 words is
  refused, which accuses no one and labels nothing. I'd leave the refusal: under
  30 words neither signal has enough to count, and a site should show no label
  on a post the check can't read.
- **Id rotation walks past the rate limit** (Rate Limiting said it would be
  listed here). A script that changes `creator_id` on every request gets a
  fresh budget each time. What I'd do: add a loose per-address ceiling above
  the per-creator limit, high enough for a shared dorm network, with the eval's
  localhost address exempted. Why I stopped: there are no accounts to tie a
  budget to, and who shares an address budget is a design decision, not a
  one-line change.
- **Every response hands back both signal scores.** That tells a probing script
  how far it is from each line, and the rate limit only slows it down. What I'd
  do: give readers the label only and keep the scores for reviewers on
  `GET /content`. Why I stopped: unit 7's contract requires `model_score` and
  `style_score` in the `/submit` response.
- **Wrong-type text gets the wrong reason.** `"text": 12345` is refused with
  `missing_text` ("There's no text to look at"). What I'd do: a separate
  `invalid_text` reason. Why I stopped: the refusal is still clean and logged,
  and it is a route change.

**What unit 7 said would come here**

- **A flagged writer gets no reason.** Unit 7's second agent asked for reasons
  in plain terms. FP03 shows why: the true reason is that GPT-2 finds the
  writer's English predictable, and the label can't say that. What I'd do: one
  sentence on an AI label naming which measures drove it, read back by a fresh
  agent as the writer, the way the labels were. Why I stopped: a reason that
  helps a writer also tells a script what to change, so it is its own design
  problem.
- **No recorded outcome when a review withdraws a flag.** An appeal sets
  `under_review`, and nothing closes it. What I'd do: a reviewer route that
  records `upheld` or `withdrawn` and changes the label. Why I stopped: it needs
  reviewer authentication, which this service doesn't have.
- **Second-language writing is barely tested.** Unit 7's calibration had none.
  The staff set had one text, FP03, and it was the worst outcome. A held-out
  set of second-language writing is what criterion 1 needs next.
- **The AI-label appeal is still only covered by unit tests.** Nothing in the
  eval reaches `ai`. The Sample Run essay does (0.7591), and it is the text to
  add to `run_eval.py::run_appeals`.

**The trade I'd make if this ran for real:**

I'd keep the AI band high and let more AI text through as `unsure`, because a
wrong `ai` label lands on one named writer while a missed one is spread thin
across every reader. Readers pay for that with less warning, and so do honest
writers, whose real work sits beside AI text that carries the same `unsure`
label they do.

## Capstone

**What This Extends**

Provenance Guard already scores submissions and records decisions and appeals. The capstone adds independent HTTP request records so a response and the time spent producing it can be traced after the process exits.

**The Shape**

Shape A: deploy and instrument. A local single-worker server exposes the existing app with a separate request log for `POST /submit`. Classifier training, score thresholds, appeal workflow changes, and public hosting are out of scope.

**Running It**

Path B, local with logs. [RUNNING.md](RUNNING.md#capstone-clean-local-run) has the exact launch command and dependency lock. I installed it from a clean Linux checkout with Python 3.12.14, started the single Gunicorn worker from a terminal, and got HTTP 200 from a real submission. The final version was tested from a fresh checkout with the same dependency environment and cached GPT-2 revision `607a30d783dfa663caf39e06633721c8d4cfcd7e`. No hosted API was used.

**Instrumentation**

`capstone_service.py::record_submit_response` writes one JSONL event for each `POST /submit` response. It records the UTC timestamp, request ID, request body byte count, validated word count when available, response status and either the classifier outcome or a rejection reason. The response's `X-Request-ID` header matches the log. The request log omits raw text and creator IDs; decisions and appeals are still handled by the existing audit. The duration fields run from the Flask request hook to immediately before the log append, so they exclude disk append and network transit.

**What's Still Broken**

None of the five locked criteria missed. I'd tighten criterion 3 by adding one ordinary rate-limit rejection to each trial and requiring **4 of 4 correctly recorded rejections**. One observed 429 is useful evidence, but it's not three separate repetitions. This deployment stays single-worker and local: the append lock is process-local, and if the log path cannot be written the response still returns while the worker reports an error. I stopped before trying to claim multi-worker safety or durability on disk failure.
